Imagine it's 2028. A startup launches with one employee. You.

No team. No office. Just a laptop, a strong idea, and an army of AI agents working while you sleep.

They answer support tickets, write the code, chase invoices, and ship the updates. By morning, your company has done a week of work.

At first, it feels like magic. Then it feels like leverage. One person, doing what used to take fifty. A solo founder with a clear system can now ship like a team of twenty, and pivot before a committee finishes its first meeting.

The tools are already here. The agents are getting better every month. The cost of starting is collapsing. And the old advantage, headcount, is becoming a liability. That doesn't make people worthless. It makes coordination cheap, and judgment scarce.

But here's the part nobody puts in the demo.

Automation can multiply a good decision. It can also multiply a bad one, faster than anyone can catch it. One careless prompt can become a thousand bad emails, or a headline.

So the skill that matters isn't typing prompts. It's knowing which decisions should never be delegated.

The one-person company is coming. The question is not whether you can run it alone. It's whether you'll know what still needs a human. Not because you can do everything. Because you know what not to automate.

That's the future worth building.
